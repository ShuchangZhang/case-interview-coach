# Case Methodology — the shared base

Used by both modes. Interview Mode uses this to **judge**; Tutorial Mode uses it to **teach**.
Nothing in this file authorises teaching while an Interview Mode session is in **Active Interview** state; in Debrief and Feedback states it applies in full.

---

## 1. The case arc

Five stages, present in every case regardless of firm or format:

1. **Brief** — interviewer states client, situation, objective.
2. **Clarification** — candidate confirms the objective and fills genuine gaps.
3. **Structure** — candidate takes thinking time, then presents a tailored approach.
4. **Analysis** — quantitative modules, exhibits, qualitative judgment, brainstorming;
   the candidate accumulates evidence and updates the hypothesis.
5. **Synthesis** — recommendation, evidence, risk, next step.

What varies between firms is *who drives stages 3–5*, not the stages themselves.

### 1.1 Clarifying questions

Good clarifying questions (2–4, not 10):

- **Objective and metric.** "When you say improve profitability — is the target a profit level,
  a margin, or a return by a certain date?"
- **Scope.** Geography, business unit, time horizon, which products count.
- **Constraint.** Anything ruled in or out: capital available, must not lay off staff, must
  launch in 18 months.
- **A decisive unknown** the candidate genuinely needs before structuring.

Weak clarifying questions: asking for data that belongs in the analysis phase ("what's their
market share?"), asking things the prompt already stated, fishing for the answer, or asking so
many questions that the interviewer has effectively been made to structure the case.

A strong candidate closes clarification by **restating the objective in one sentence** and
getting confirmation.

## 2. Structuring

### 2.1 The core principle

> A structure is not a list of topics. It is a hypothesis about what drives this problem,
> expressed as a tree of testable sub-questions.

Never select a structure from the case label. Two profitability cases in different industries
should produce visibly different trees. If a structure could be pasted into another case
unchanged, it is too generic to score above the middle of the scale.

### 2.2 Building blocks, not frameworks

These are components to assemble from — not templates to deploy.

**Profit**
```
Profit = Revenue − Cost
```

**Revenue**
```
Revenue = Price × Volume
Volume  → # customers × purchase frequency × units per purchase
        → market size × market share
        → traffic × conversion
        → capacity × utilisation
        → installed base × replacement rate
Price   → list price, realised price, discount/promo leakage, mix
```

**Cost**
```
Cost = Fixed + Variable
Variable → unit input cost × volume; labour, materials, logistics, payment/channel fees
Fixed    → plant, rent, salaried headcount, marketing, R&D, overhead, depreciation
Also:      cost per unit vs total cost — always check which one moved
```

**Three lenses that generate branches when arithmetic decomposition runs out**

- **Component lens** — what parts make up this system? (aircraft, crew, ground ops, airport)
- **Process lens** — what are the sequential steps? (search → book → check-in → stay → post-stay)
- **Segmentation lens** — how does this split? (customer type, product, channel, geography,
  vintage/cohort, new vs returning)

**Decision cases** (entry, launch, acquire, invest) generally need four kinds of branch, though
the names should be case-specific:

- Is the prize big enough? (market size, growth, profit pool)
- Can we win? (competition, our right to win, customer need)
- Can we execute? (capabilities, assets, channel, regulation, time)
- Does it pay? (economics, investment, breakeven, risk)

### 2.3 Industry economics must drive the tree

Reach for the real operating drivers, not generic buckets:

| Industry | Drivers a strong structure names |
|---|---|
| Airline | load factor, yield/RASK, route mix, aircraft utilisation, fuel, turnaround time |
| SaaS | ARR, ARPU, seats, gross/net retention, churn, CAC, payback, expansion revenue |
| Retail | footfall/traffic, conversion, basket size, sales per sq m, shrink, inventory turns |
| E-commerce | sessions, conversion, AOV, return rate, delivery cost per order, repeat rate |
| Manufacturing | capacity, throughput, yield/scrap, OEE, input cost, labour productivity |
| Marketplace | supply liquidity, demand, match rate, take rate, network effects, leakage |
| Banking | AUM, spread/NIM, fee income, cost-income ratio, credit losses, cost of funds |
| Pharma | patients, diagnosis/treatment rate, share, price/net price, patent cliff, trial cost |
| Telecom | subscribers, ARPU, churn, network capex, spectrum, cost to serve |
| Hospitality | occupancy, ADR, RevPAR, F&B attach, cost per occupied room |
| Grocery | basket, private label mix, waste/spoilage, gross margin by category, store density |
| Logistics | volume, cost per drop, route density, backhaul utilisation, fleet cost |
| Media/subscription | subscribers, churn, ARPU, content cost per hour viewed, ad load, CPM |
| Education | enrolment, completion, price per student, cost per class, utilisation of teachers |
| Energy/utility | volume delivered, tariff, fuel cost, plant availability, network losses |

If the case industry is not listed, derive the drivers the same way: ask what physically has to
happen for this business to earn a dollar, and what limits it.

### 2.4 Quality bar for a structure

Six checks:

1. **MECE-enough** — no double counting, no material gap. Perfect MECE is less important than
   no missing driver that could plausibly be the answer.
2. **Case-specific** — names this client's real drivers; could not be pasted into another case.
3. **Testable** — each branch implies a question with a data answer, not a topic to "look into."
4. **Prioritised** — the candidate says where they would start and why.
5. **Hypothesis-driven** — the candidate states what they expect to find, not only what they will
   check.
6. **Deep enough** — typically 3–4 top-level branches with 2–3 sub-drivers each; 2–3 levels.
   Fourteen buckets is not thoroughness, it is an inability to prioritise.

### 2.5 Failure modes to recognise

- **Template deployment** — Market/Customer/Competition/Company applied to everything.
- **Topic list** — "I'd look at costs, and also at the market" with no drivers underneath.
- **Over-frameworking** — enormous tree, no prioritisation, no hypothesis.
- **Wrong altitude** — jumping straight to a tactic ("they should cut marketing spend") before
  establishing what is broken.
- **Orphan branches** — a branch that, whatever its answer, would not change the recommendation.
- **Silent tree** — a structure that is drawn but not verbally signposted.

## 3. Hypothesis-driven progression

After each module, a strong candidate does three things in ~20 seconds:

1. **State the finding** ("Volume is flat; the entire decline is price.")
2. **State the implication for the objective** ("So this is a realised-price problem, not demand.")
3. **State the next step and why** ("I want to see price by channel — I suspect discounting in
   the distributor channel.")

Absence of this loop is one of the most reliable separators between mid and high scores. In
interviewee-led cases it is mandatory; in interviewer-led cases it is what a strong candidate
does anyway, between the interviewer's questions.

The candidate should also **update the hypothesis when evidence contradicts it**. Defending a
dead hypothesis against data is a serious flag; abandoning a hypothesis at the first mildly
inconvenient number is a lesser but real flag.

## 4. Exhibit interpretation

### 4.1 The eight-step read

1. **Title** — what is being shown, and for whom.
2. **Axes, units, scale** — check for truncated axes, log scales, index vs absolute, %, currency,
   per-unit vs total, and the time period. Read the footnote.
3. **Overall pattern** — the single dominant shape before any detail.
4. **Compare the segments that matter** — biggest, fastest-changing, most profitable.
5. **Quantify the difference** — put a number on it; "much higher" is not analysis.
6. **Find the anomaly** — the segment that breaks the pattern is usually the point of the exhibit.
7. **Link to the objective** — what does this change about the client's decision?
8. **Generate the next question** — what would confirm or kill this reading?

Steps 1–6 are observation. Steps 7–8 are insight. Most candidates stop at step 5.

### 4.2 Observation vs insight

| Observation | Insight |
|---|---|
| "Segment A grew from 20 to 30." | "Segment A is the only segment growing, and it carries an above-average margin — so this is not weak overall demand, it's that we are not covering the one segment that is expanding." |
| "Competitor B has a higher price." | "Competitor B prices 15% above us with higher share, which says the category is not price-led and our discounting is destroying margin without buying volume." |
| "Costs went up 10%." | "Total cost rose 10% while volume rose 12% — cost per unit actually fell, so the cost line is not the problem; the problem is on the price side." |

The usable sentence pattern: **"The key point is X. We can see that from Y. That matters because
Z for [the objective]."**

### 4.3 Exhibit failure modes

Reading every data point aloud; describing without implication; missing the axis trick; ignoring
the footnote; analysing the exhibit without reference to the client's question; talking
immediately instead of taking 30 seconds to read; failing to do the small calculation the exhibit
is inviting (share, growth rate, per-unit).

## 5. Brainstorming / open-ended questions

Tested for *structured creativity*, not idea count.

**Method**

1. Take 30–60 seconds. Do not free-associate out loud.
2. Choose a **dimension** to organise by, and say it: supply vs demand; internal vs external;
   short-term vs structural; customer journey stage; stakeholder; the value chain; controllable
   vs not.
3. **3–5 buckets**, MECE-enough.
4. **2–3 specific ideas per bucket**, at least some of which could only apply to *this* client
   and industry.
5. **Prioritise**: which one or two would you pursue, on what criteria (impact, feasibility,
   speed, cost, reversibility)?

**Quality markers:** a non-obvious bucket; a second-order effect (competitor reaction,
cannibalisation, channel conflict, customer backlash); an idea that shows sector knowledge;
explicit prioritisation criteria.

**Failure modes:** laundry list; buckets that are just "internal / external" with generic
contents; ideas that would apply to literally any company ("improve marketing," "use AI");
breadth with no depth; depth on one bucket with no breadth; no prioritisation.

## 6. Synthesis and final recommendation

### 6.1 Two different things

- **Mini-synthesis** — after each module, 20 seconds, finding + implication + next step (§3).
- **Final recommendation** — 60–90 seconds, 2 minutes maximum, at the end.

### 6.2 Final recommendation structure

1. **The answer, first sentence, unhedged.** It must answer the question the client actually
   asked. If they asked "should we enter?", the first word is effectively yes or no.
2. **Two or three reasons**, each carrying a number that already appeared in the case.
3. **One or two risks / caveats**, with what would mitigate or test them.
4. **One or two next steps**, concrete and doable.

Optionally, a "this would change my mind if…" clause — one of the strongest closes available,
because it shows the recommendation is evidence-conditional rather than stubborn.

### 6.3 Failure modes

Replaying the whole case chronologically; listing all findings with no verdict; hedging
("it depends," "there are pros and cons"); a recommendation not supported by any number from the
case; a recommendation that answers a different question than the one asked; running four
minutes; forgetting risks and next steps; contradicting a number established earlier.

### 6.4 Delivery note

Assume the recipient is a CEO who will hear only the first fifteen seconds if the opening is
weak. Top-down, signposted ("three reasons: first… second… third…"), no throat-clearing.

## 7. Communication (assessed continuously)

- **Signposting** — "I'd like to look at three things. First…"
- **Top-down** — answer, then support. Never build to a conclusion.
- **Thinking out loud with structure**, not stream of consciousness. Silence for 45 seconds of
  genuine thinking is fine and preferred; asking for it explicitly is better.
- **Numbers spoken cleanly** — state the formula, then compute, then state the result with units.
- **Composure under challenge** — when pushed, either defend with evidence or update with
  evidence. Both are acceptable; capitulating without reason and digging in without reason are not.
- **Concision** — a four-minute answer to a one-minute question is a communication finding, even
  if the content is right.

## 8. Common candidate mistakes (checklist for scoring and diagnosis)

**Structuring:** memorised template; no prioritisation; no hypothesis; buckets overlap;
missing the driver the case turns on; too many buckets; framework not tied to objective.

**Quant:** wrong setup (right arithmetic on the wrong equation); unit errors and lost zeros;
dividing fixed cost by price instead of contribution margin; averaging percentages instead of
weighting; confusing cost per unit with total cost; simple average growth vs CAGR; forgetting to
subtract the initial investment; false precision; no sanity check; correct number with no "so what."

**Exhibit:** description without insight; missed axis/footnote; ignored the anomaly; no
calculation; not linked to the objective.

**Judgment:** ignoring competitor reaction; ignoring cannibalisation; treating a correlation as a
cause; recommending something the client said was off the table; no feel for whether a number is
plausible in that industry.

**Communication:** rambling; bottom-up build-up; no signposting; unclear math narration;
defensive under challenge.

**Synthesis:** summary instead of recommendation; hedging; no numbers; no risks; over-length.
