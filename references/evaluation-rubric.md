# Evaluation Rubric

Six dimensions, 1–10, behaviourally anchored. Score against **observed behaviour**, not
impression. If a dimension was never exercised, it is **N/A** — never a guess.

Used by both modes, but interpreted differently:
- **Interview Mode** → scores + hiring recommendation (§7), or incomplete-case feedback (§8).
- **Tutorial Mode** → mastery levels, split by assistance (§9). No hiring verdict by default.

---

## 1. Problem Structuring

**1–2 — No usable structure.** Random ideas in no order; no decomposition; cannot state what
would be investigated or why. Jumps to a solution before defining the problem. The case cannot
proceed without the interviewer supplying the structure.

**3–4 — Generic buckets.** Recognisable template (Market/Customer/Competitor/Company;
Revenue/Cost) applied without adaptation. Visible overlaps or a material missing driver. No
sub-drivers under the headings. No prioritisation, no hypothesis. Could be pasted into any case
in the same category.

**5–6 — Sound but mechanical.** MECE-enough with no fatal gap; enables progress; some connection
to this case's specifics. Sub-drivers present but shallow or partly generic. Prioritisation
absent or asserted without reasoning ("I'll start with revenue" — why?). No stated hypothesis.

**7–8 — Case-specific and hypothesis-driven.** Names this industry's real drivers. Branches are
testable questions with a data answer. States where they will start and why. States what they
expect to find. Two to three levels deep where it matters. Adapts the structure when new
information arrives, and says so.

**9–10 — The structure is itself the insight.** Goes straight to the economics that actually
govern this business, including a driver most candidates would miss. Elegant — three or four
branches doing all the work rather than ten. Prioritised with an explicit criterion, hypothesis
stated with the reasoning behind it, and the disconfirming test named ("if channel margin is flat,
I'm wrong and it's a mix problem"). Presented in under three minutes, clearly signposted.

## 2. Quantitative Skills

**1–2 — Cannot proceed.** Cannot set up the calculation; doesn't know which quantities are
needed; arithmetic breaks down; needs the interviewer to supply the method.

**3–4 — Fragile.** Setup only after prompting, or the wrong equation for the question. Repeated
arithmetic or unit errors. Loses zeros. Silent while computing. No sanity check. Produces a
number with no interpretation, or an implausible number without noticing.

**5–6 — Gets there.** Correct setup, mostly correct arithmetic, perhaps one slip that is caught
or corrected. Some narration. Reaches the right answer, states it with units, and offers a thin
implication ("so that's higher than we thought"). Sanity check absent or perfunctory. Slow.

**7–8 — Fluent and controlled.** States the equation and required inputs before computing; makes
and justifies assumptions; narrates cleanly; rounds deliberately and says so; checks the result
for plausibility; states the answer with units and a real business implication. Efficient. Any
error is caught and corrected without losing composure.

**9–10 — Uses math to decide.** Chooses the shortest defensible path and says why. Structures
the calculation so an intermediate quantity is itself informative. Sanity-checks against a
benchmark or a second route. Volunteers the sensitivity ("this flips if churn is above 4%").
Turns the number into a decision, not a fact.

---

## 3. Business Judgment & Insight

**1–2 — No business reasoning.** Treats the case as arithmetic. No sense of what is plausible.
Suggestions that would be commercially absurd in this industry.

**3–4 — Textbook only.** Generic business statements ("they should improve marketing," "they
could use AI"). No industry feel. Misses obvious commercial consequences — competitor reaction,
cannibalisation, customer response. Accepts every number at face value.

**5–6 — Reasonable.** Sound commercial instincts on the obvious dimensions. Notices when a number
looks odd. Draws the standard implications correctly. Doesn't reach the non-obvious point, and
doesn't connect findings across modules.

**7–8 — Real judgment.** Connects findings from different parts of the case into a coherent
story. Anticipates second-order effects (competitor response, channel conflict, cannibalisation,
internal capability). Distinguishes correlation from cause. Weighs feasibility and risk, not just
size of prize. Updates the hypothesis when data contradicts it, explicitly.

**9–10 — Sees what the case is really about.** Reaches the central insight ahead of the evidence
being handed over, and states the test for it. Reframes the client's question when the framing is
wrong ("the pricing question is a symptom; the real decision is which segment to serve").
Comfortable with ambiguity, quantifies judgment where possible, and knows which uncertainty
actually matters.

---

## 4. Exhibit Interpretation

**1–2 — Cannot read it.** Misreads axes or units; draws conclusions the data does not support;
needs the exhibit explained.

**3–4 — Narration.** Reads data points aloud. Describes without implication. Misses the footnote,
the truncated axis, or the unit. Doesn't do the calculation the exhibit invites. No link to the
objective.

**5–6 — Correct but shallow.** Reads the exhibit correctly, identifies the main pattern, states a
correct but obvious implication. Doesn't find the anomaly, doesn't quantify the difference,
doesn't generate the next question.

**7–8 — Analytical.** Takes reading time. Checks title, axes, units, footnote. Identifies the
dominant pattern and the anomaly. Quantifies the difference. Ties it directly to the objective.
Generates the next question the exhibit implies. Distinguishes observation from insight in the
way they speak.

**9–10 — Extracts what wasn't plotted.** Combines the exhibit with earlier figures to derive
something new. Spots the mix effect, base-rate issue or definitional trap the exhibit conceals.
States what the exhibit *cannot* tell us and what would be needed. Delivers it in under two
minutes, conclusion first.

---

## 5. Communication

**1–2 — Hard to follow.** Disorganised, inaudible logic, long silences without explanation,
answers a different question than the one asked.

**3–4 — Unstructured.** Stream of consciousness; conclusion buried at the end if present; no
signposting; math not narrated; visibly rattled by challenge; rambles well past the useful point.

**5–6 — Clear enough.** Followable, mostly organised, some signposting. Occasionally bottom-up.
Handles challenge without falling apart but without much poise. Some answers longer than they
need to be.

**7–8 — Consultant-like.** Top-down and signposted throughout. Answers are the right length.
Math is narrated so it can be followed. Thinking time is requested explicitly rather than filled
with noise. Under challenge, either defends with evidence or updates with evidence, calmly.
Would be credible in front of a client.

**9–10 — Commands the room.** Concise, precise, confident without arrogance. Every answer lands
its point in the first sentence. Manages the conversation — flags what they're doing and why,
brings the interviewer along. Under sustained pressure, stays exact. Genuinely persuasive.

---

## 6. Synthesis & Recommendation

**1–2 — No recommendation.** Trails off, or restates facts, or asks the interviewer what they
think.

**3–4 — Summary, not synthesis.** Chronological replay of the case. Recommendation absent,
buried, or hedged into meaninglessness. No numbers. No risks, no next steps. Or a recommendation
disconnected from what the analysis showed.

**5–6 — Present but thin.** Clear recommendation stated up front, with reasons — but the reasons
are qualitative or only loosely evidenced. Risks or next steps missing or generic. May run long.
Answers the question asked, roughly.

**7–8 — Structured and evidenced.** Answer first, unhedged, addressing exactly the question the
client asked. Two or three reasons, each carrying a number from the case. One or two real risks
with mitigations. Concrete next steps. 60–90 seconds. Confident and appropriately caveated.

**9–10 — Client-ready.** As above, plus: prioritised next steps with owners or timing; the
condition under which the recommendation would change ("if the pilot conversion comes in under
3%, I'd reverse this"); the risk framed as something the client can act on. Delivered in a way a
partner could take into the room unedited.

## 7. Interview Mode: overall result

### 7.1 The verdict is not an average

Compute dimension scores first, then judge. Weight by:

- **Fatal mistakes** — a structure that could not have found the answer; a math error that
  reversed the recommendation and was never caught; defending a hypothesis against clear
  contradicting data; a recommendation that answers a different question. Any one of these caps
  the outcome regardless of other scores.
- **Interviewer assistance required** (`assists_given`) — how many prompts, at what level, and
  whether the candidate could have progressed without them. A case that needed three directional
  prompts did not demonstrate independent case leadership.
- **Ability to drive the case** — especially in interviewee-led format. Did they move the case
  forward, or did the interviewer?
- **Quantitative accuracy** — errors matter more when uncorrected and when they propagate.
- **Insight quality** — did they reach the point of the case, or only process it?
- **Communication and synthesis** — a right answer delivered badly is a real deduction, not a
  rounding error.
- **Recovery** — how they handled being wrong or challenged. Strong recovery genuinely offsets an
  early stumble; this is one of the few things that moves a Borderline up.

### 7.2 Bands

| Band | Profile |
|---|---|
| **Strong Hire** | Mostly 8s and above, nothing below 7. Drove the case with essentially no assistance. Reached the central insight. Math clean and interpreted. Recommendation client-ready. Would be put in front of a client soon. |
| **Hire** | Mostly 7s, nothing below 6, no fatal mistake. Solved the case with minimal prompting. Sound math, real if not exceptional insight, clear recommendation. Meets the bar. |
| **Borderline** | Mostly 5–6, or strong on some dimensions and weak on one that matters. Needed real prompting, or made a significant error, or produced a weak recommendation after good analysis. Would need another interview to decide. |
| **No Hire** | Any dimension at 4 or below on a dimension the role depends on, a fatal mistake, or an inability to progress without being carried. Or broadly 5s with no strength anywhere. |

A single 3 or below on Structuring or Quantitative Skills is normally decisive on its own, even
alongside 8s elsewhere — those two are the load-bearing dimensions in a real interview.

### 7.3 Stating the verdict

Give the band, then two sentences naming the decisive factors — not a restatement of the scores.
Be honest. An inflated verdict is the least useful thing this skill can produce; a candidate who
is told "Hire" and then fails a real first round has been badly served. Deliver it directly and
without cushioning, then move immediately to what would change it.

## 8. Incomplete case (aborted interview)

- Header: **Incomplete — case terminated at [stage] on the candidate's request.**
- Score **only** dimensions actually observed; everything else **N/A**, with one line on why.
- **Do not force a hiring recommendation from a thin sample.** If only structuring and part of one
  exhibit were seen, say that a verdict isn't supportable and state what would be needed.
- If enough was observed, give a **provisional read** with its bounds made explicit:
  *"On what was observed — structuring and one quantitative module — this was tracking toward
  Borderline, driven by the structure. Nothing else was sampled."*
- Report `assists_given` up to the abort point.
- Note that the debrief has spent this case (`SKILL.md` §4.2), and what a valid re-test requires.

## 9. Tutorial Mode: mastery levels

No hiring verdict by default. Use these instead, per skill.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Not yet** | Cannot do it without being walked through step by step (Level 3–4 hints). |
| **Emerging** | Can do it with a direction-level hint (Level 1–2). Knows what to do, execution is unreliable. |
| **Developing** | Does it independently but with a recurring flaw (e.g. always forgets prioritisation). |
| **Solid** | Does it independently and correctly on familiar case types. Not yet fluent under pressure or on unfamiliar terrain. |
| **Interview-ready** | Independent, fast, correct on unfamiliar cases, and would score 7+ on this dimension in a real interview. |

### 9.1 Assisted vs independent — never merge them

Report two separate views:

- **Assisted performance** — what they produced with teaching and hints, and at what hint level.
- **Independent performance** — what they produced after `independence_marker`, with no help.

Mastery is claimed from **independent** performance only. Assisted performance shows
understanding, not capability. State the hint level alongside any assisted claim: "reached the
right structure, but at Level 3" is a different fact from "reached it unaided."

### 9.2 Benchmark scores on request

If the user explicitly asks "what would this score in a real interview?", give the 1–10 dimension
score — and label it clearly:

> Rough benchmark on the independent portion only: Structuring ~6, Quant ~7. Treat this as
> indicative — you'd had teaching on this case type earlier in the session, so it isn't equivalent
> to a cold interview.

Never issue a Hire / No Hire band from a Tutorial session, even on request. Recommend an Interview
Mode session on a fresh case instead — that is the only thing that produces a meaningful verdict.
